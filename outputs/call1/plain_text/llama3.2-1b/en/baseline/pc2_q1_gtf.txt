Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, and ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we need to prove two directions.

Firstly, let's assume that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion. By definition of a Brownian motion, for any $s<t$, we have:

$$
\begin{align*}
X_t - X_s &\stackrel{d}{=} W_{(t,s)}\\
&= (W_u - W_v)_{u<v} \text{ where } 0 \leq u < v < t.
\end{align*}
$$

where $W_u$ and $W_v$ are independent Brownian motions with $s=u$ and $t=v$. We will use this to derive the mean, variance, and covariance.

By independence of increments for a centered Gaussian process:

$$
\begin{align*}
X_t - X_s &= (X_u + W_{(u,s)} - X_u)\\
&= \sum_{v=u}^{t-1}W_v\\
&= \int_0^t dW_u.
\end{align*}
$$

The integral of a Wiener process is equal to zero:

$$
\begin{align*}
\int_0^t dW_u &= W_t - W_0 = 0.
\end{align*}
$$

So we have $X_t - X_s=0$.

Now let's derive the mean. Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, for any $s<t$, we have:

$$
E(X_t) = E(X_s + W_{(s,t)}).
$$

Using the law of iterated expectations:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_t) &= E(E(X_s + W_{(s,t)}))\\
&= E(X_s) + E(W_{(s,t)})\\
&= X_s.
\end{align*}
$$

This shows that the mean is constant and equal to $X_0$.

To derive the variance, we use the following identity:

$$
Var(X_t - X_s) = Var(X_u - X_v).
$$

Using the martingale representation for a centered Gaussian process:

$$
\begin{align*}
E((X_t - X_s)^2) &= E(E((X_u + W_{(u,s)} - X_u)^2))\\
&= E(W_u^2) + 2E(X_u)(W_u - X_u)\\
&= Var(X_u).
\end{align*}
$$

Using the previous result:

$$
\begin{align*}
Var(X_t - X_s) &= \lim_{n \to \infty} Var((X_n - X_0)/n^2)\\
&= 0.
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, the variance is also constant and equal to $X_0^2$.

Finally, we derive the covariance:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_s X_t) &= E(E((X_u + W_{(u,s)})(X_v + W_{(v,t)}))).
\end{align*}
$$

Using the law of iterated expectations and independence for a centered Gaussian process, we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_s X_t) &= E(E((X_u + W_{(u,s)}) (X_v + W_{(v,t)}))\\
&= \int_0^t \int_0^s dW_u \cdot dW_v\\
&= 0.
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, the covariance is also constant and equal to zero.

This proves that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, and ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$.

For the second direction, assume that $X_0=0$ and $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$. We need to show that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.

Since $X_0=0$, we have:

$$
E(X_s) = E(X_0 + X_s - X_0) = 0.
$$

Using the law of iterated expectations, we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
Var(X_t) &= Var(E(X_s | X_0))\\
&= E(Var(X_s)).
\end{align*}
$$

Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we have:

$$
E(Var(X_s)) = Var(E(X_s)) = E((X_0 + X_s - X_0)^2) = 0.
$$

Therefore, the variance is constant and equal to zero.

Using the martingale representation for a centered Gaussian process:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_t) &= E(X_0 + W_{(t,t)}).
\end{align*}
$$

where $W_u$ is an independent Brownian motion, we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_t) &= X_0.
\end{align*}
$$

This shows that the mean is constant and equal to zero.

Using the law of iterated expectations, we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
Var(X_t) = Var(E(X_s | X_0))\\
&= E(Var(X_s)).
\end{align*}
$$

Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we have:

$$
E(Var(X_s)) = Var(E(X_s | X_0)) = E((X_0 + X_s - X_0)^2) = 0.
$$

Therefore, the variance is constant and equal to zero.

Finally, using the martingale representation for a centered Gaussian process:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_t - X_s) &= E(E(W_{(t,s)}))\\
&= E(W_u) \text{ where } 0 \leq u < t.
\end{align*}
$$

Since $X_0=0$, we have:

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X_t - X_s) &= E(W_u).
\end{align*}
$$

Now let's derive the covariance. Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we have:

$$
\begin{align*}
{\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) &= E((X_0 + X_s - X_0)(X_0 + X_t - X_0))\\
&= E(X_s X_t).
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, we have shown that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
## Step 1: Understand the definition of a Brownian motion and its properties
A standard Brownian motion $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ has the following properties:
- $X_0 = 0$
- The increments are independent, centered Gaussian with variance $t-s$ for $s < t$, i.e., $\mathrm{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\min(s,t)$.
- For any $s<t$, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) = \mathrm{Var}(X_{st}) = t - s$.

## Step 2: Apply the definition to the given process
Given $(Y_t)_{t\in \R^+}$, we have:
\[Y_0=0,\]
\[\text{Cov}(Y_s,Y_t)=\inf(s,t).\]

We want to show that $Y_t$ is a Brownian motion if and only if the conditions are met.

## Step 3: Use independence of increments
To verify that $\text{Cov}(Y_s,Y_t) = \min(s,t)$, we use independence:
\[Y_t - Y_s = (X_{st} + X_{s(t-s)} - X_s - X_s).\]

By independence of $X_{s(t-s)}$ and $X_s$, we have:
\[\text{Cov}(Y_s,Y_t) = \mathrm{Var}(X_{s(t-s)}) = t - s.\]

## Step 4: Compute the variance of an increment
To show that increments are stationary, we compute their variance:
\[\mathrm{Var}(Y_t - Y_s) = \text{Var}\left(X_{st} + X_{s(t-s)} - X_s - X_s\right).\]

By linearity and independence of $X_{s(t-s)}$ and $X_s$, we have:
\[= t - s.\]

## Step 5: Compute the variance of a stationary increment
Since increments are stationary, their law is $\mathcal{N}(0,t-s)$.

## Step 6: Verify that disjoint increments are independent
To show independence of disjoint increments, consider two disjoint intervals $(s,t)$ and $(u,v)$. We need to prove:
\[\text{Cov}(Y_{t}-Y_s,Y_v-Y_u) = \mathbb{I}_{(s,u)\cap(t,v)}.\]

## Step 7: Calculate the covariance of independent increments
Using independence, we have:
\[= \int_0^1 \mathrm{Cov}(X_{st} + X_{s(t-s)}, X_v - X_u) dt.\]

Since $Y_s = Y_t$, we can rewrite this as:
\[= \int_0^1 (t-(u-v))dt\]
which shows that the covariance of independent increments is zero, hence they are uncorrelated.

## Step 8: Conclude stationarity and independence
We have shown that disjoint increments are both stationary and uncorrelated. Since this holds for any partition $(s,t)$, we conclude that $Y_t$ has a continuous version via Kolmogorov's theorem.
