Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$ and $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \inf(s, t)$, we proceed by induction on $n$.

First, consider the base case $n = 1$. A standard Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ satisfies $X_0 = 0$ and $\text{Cov}(B_s, B_t) = s - t$, which is precisely the condition given. Thus, we have verified the base case.

Assume now that the result holds for all processes of dimension up to $n-1$. We need to show it also holds for a process of dimension $n$.

Consider a standard Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$. By the multivariate central limit theorem, we have:
$$
\text{Cov}(B_s, B_t) = s - t.
$$

Using Ito's formula, we can write $X_t$ as an expectation of a deterministic process. Applying the result from the base case to this deterministic process, and taking expectations, we obtain that $(X_t)$ is a centered Gaussian process.

Now, let's show that if $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ satisfies $\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, then it is a Brownian motion. Since $X_0 = 0$ by assumption, we can apply Ito's formula to obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dX_t &= d(X_t) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 X_t dt \\
&= X_t \cdot (dt + \sigma^2 dt)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\sigma > 0$ is the volatility of $X$. Define a new process $Y_s = s - t$. Then, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dX_s &= d(X_s) \\
&= X_s Y_s ds + \sigma^2 X_s dt
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\text{Cov}(X_s,Y_s)=s$ by assumption. Rearranging terms and applying Ito's formula again, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dY_s &= d(Y_s) \\
&= Y_s (\text{Var} X_t) ds + 2\sigma^2 Y_s dt
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\text{Cov}(X_s,Y_t)=\inf(s,t)$ by assumption. Since $Y_0 = 0$, we can apply the strong Markov property to deduce that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dY_s &= d(Y_s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let's show that $\text{Cov}(X_t,Y_t) = \inf(t,t)$ for all $t$. Using Ito's formula again, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Cov}(X_t, Y_t) &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (\partial_0 X_t)^2 + 2\sigma^2 \sqrt{\frac{1-2\sigma^2}{4\pi t}} (Y_t)_t
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\partial_0$ denotes the partial derivative with respect to $X$. Since $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a standard Brownian motion, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(B_t)_t &= t
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Cov}(X_t,Y_t) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{2} + 0 = \sigma^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $\text{Cov}(X_t,Y_t)=\inf(t,t)$ for all $t$. This shows that the process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
$$
dX_t = d(X_t) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (X_t - X_0)^2 dt \\
= d(X_t) + \sigma^2 X_t dt
$$

where $\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$ by assumption.

Since $W_{s-t}$ is a standard Brownian motion, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dW_{s-t} &= d(W_{s-t}) \\
&= W_{s-t} dt + \sigma^2 (W_{s-t}-X_0)^2 ds
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranging terms and applying Ito's formula again, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dW_s &= d(W_s) \\
&= W_s dt + 2\sigma^2 W_s (W_s - X_0)
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $X_0 = 0$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dW_s &= d(W_s) \\
&= W_s dt + \sigma^2 (W_s)^2 ds
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying Ito's formula again, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dX_t &= X_t d(W_t) \\
&= X_t \left( W_t dt + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (W_t)^2 dt - tdt \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dY_t &= d(Y_t) \\
&= Y_t (\text{Var} X_t) dt + 2\sigma^2 Y_t (W_t)^2 ds
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\text{Cov}(X_s,Y_t)=s$ by assumption.

Since $X_0 = 0$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y_0 &= 0 \\
dY_0 &= d(Y_0) \\
&= Y_0 (\text{Var} X_t) dt + \sigma^2 Y_0 (W_t)^2 ds - tdt
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dY_t &= d(Y_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the strong Markov property to deduce that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dX_t &= X_t \left( W_t dt + \sigma^2 (W_t)^2 dt - tdt \right) \\
&= X_t Y_t dt + 2\sigma^2 X_t Y_t (W_t)^2 ds
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.

Therefore, the process $\left(\frac{X_t}{t}\; W_{1/t}, t>0,X_0=0 \right)$ is a Brownian motion.
