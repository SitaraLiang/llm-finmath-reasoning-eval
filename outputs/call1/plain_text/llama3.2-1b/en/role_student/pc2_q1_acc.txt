Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, and ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we need to prove two directions:

\begin{align*}
& \text{(i) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a Brownian motion, then } X_0=0\\
& \Rightarrow & (X_0)_{t\in \R^+} = 0 \\
& \text{ and } \\ 
& \text{(ii) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a centered Gaussian process, then ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)}\\
& \Rightarrow & {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) = 0
\end{align*}

Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion, we have that $X_0=X_1=X_2=\cdots$ and ${\rm Var}(X_t)=t$. We also know that ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)={\rm Var}(X_s-{\rm E}(X_s))$, where ${\rm E}(X_s)$ is the mean of $X_s$. For a Brownian motion, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{(i) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a Brownian motion, then } X_0=0\\
& \Rightarrow & {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)={\rm Var}(X_s) \\
& = s
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $s=t$, we have that ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=s=s=t$. This implies that ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=0$.

Conversely, suppose $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process. Then for any $s,t>0$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{(ii) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a centered Gaussian process, then ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=0}\\
& \Rightarrow & {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) = 0 \\
& \end{aligned}
$$

This implies that ${\rm Var}(X_s-{\rm E}(X_s))=0$. Since $s$ and $t$ are arbitrary, we can conclude that ${\rm Var}(X_s)={\rm Var}(X_t)=0$. This implies that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process.

Now, suppose that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion. Then we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{(i) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a Brownian motion, then } X_0=0\\
& \Rightarrow & {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)={\rm Var}(X_s-{\rm E}(X_s)) = s - {\rm E}(X_s)^2 \\
& \Rightarrow & 0=s-s \\
& \end{aligned}
$$

This implies that ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=s$. On the other hand, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{(ii) If }(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}\text{ is a centered Gaussian process, then ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=0}\\
& \Rightarrow & {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) = 0 \\
& \end{aligned}
$$

This implies that ${\rm Var}(X_s-{\rm E}(X_s))=s-s={\rm Var}(X_s).$

Therefore, we have shown that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, and ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$.

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
## Step 1: Definition of a Brownian Motion
A stochastic process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is called a Brownian motion if it satisfies the following properties:
- It starts at zero, i.e., $X_0=0$.
- It has continuous paths.
- For any $s < t$, the increment $X_t - X_s$ is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance $t-s$.

## Step 2: Standard Brownian Motion
The standard Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ can be defined as:
\[ B_0 = 0, \quad B_t = tW_{\frac{1}{t}}, \quad t > 0, \]
where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion.

## Step 3: Central Limit Theorem
By the central limit theorem (CLT), we have:
\[ \sqrt{T}(\bar{X}_T - \mu) \stackrel{\text{d}}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0, T)\quad\forall T>0. \]

## Step 4: Convergence of Increments
For any $s < t$, we have:
\[ E[\sqrt{T}(\bar{X}_T - \mu)|\mathcal{F}_s] = 0, \]
since $\bar{X}_T$ is a constant and $\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{T}]=0$. Hence, by the strong law of large numbers (SLLN), we have:
\[ X_t - \int_s^t\sqrt{\tau}\,dW_\tau = 0, \quad t > s. \]

## Step 5: Convergence of the Process
By Steps 3 and 4, we conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ converges to zero in probability.

The final answer is: $\boxed{X_0=0,\; (X_t)_{t\in \R^+}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\int_0^s \sqrt{s'} dW_{s'}, &s<s<T\\ 0, &T\le s<+\infty. \end{matrix}\right.}$
