Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) is a Brownian motion if and only if it satisfies the following conditions:

1. \( X_0 = 0 \),
2. The process \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) is a centered Gaussian process,
3. The covariance function \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t \).

**Proof:**

**(⇒ If direction)**

Assume that \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) satisfies all three conditions.

1. **\( X_0 = 0 \):** This ensures the process starts at zero.
2. **Centered Gaussian Process:** Each finite-dimensional distribution is multivariate normal with mean zero, fulfilling the centering requirement.
3. **Covariance Function:** The covariance \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t \) implies that increments are independent and have variances proportional to their time separation.

These conditions align exactly with the defining properties of a Brownian motion:
- It is continuous in probability (pathwise).
- The increments are independent and stationary.
- The covariance structure is linear, specifically \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t \).

Thus, \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) must be a Brownian motion.

**(⇐ Only if direction)**

Suppose \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) is a Brownian motion. Then it satisfies all the standard properties:

1. **\( X_0 = 0 \):** By definition.
2. **Centered Gaussian Process:** Each finite-dimensional distribution is multivariate normal with mean zero.
3. **Covariance Function:** For any \( s < t \), the covariance \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \); otherwise, it's zero. This corresponds to \( s \wedge t \).

Since all these properties are satisfied by Brownian motion, we conclude that if a process satisfies these conditions, it must be a Brownian motion.

**Conclusion:**

A process \( (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+} \) is a Brownian motion if and only if it is centered Gaussian with \( X_0 = 0 \) and covariance function \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t \).

```lean4
theorem brownian_motion_iff (processes : 
    ℝ → ℝ → 𝓝 ↦ 𝔽) :
(∀ t ∈ ℝ^+, processes t ∈ 𝔽 ∧ Gaussian measurable)
⇒ (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+ → (fun s ↦ processes s ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun s ↦ processes s ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+
          → BrownianMotion processes))) :=
    -- Assume the process satisfies all three conditions of a Brownian motion.
    filter_upwards fun s ↦ fun t ↦ Filter.has_nonempty_range (fun f ↦ (f 0 = 0 ∧ Gaussian measurable) f (by simp [f])) at
      ⟨fun t ↦ by aesop, fun s ↦ fun t ↦ by aesop⟩
```

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
To demonstrate that \( \{X_t = tW_{1/t},\ t > 0,\ X_0 = 0\} \) is a Brownian motion when \( W_t \) is, we proceed as follows:

**Proof:**

We need to verify that the process \( X_t = tW_{1/t} \) for \( t > 0 \), with \( X_0 = 0 \), satisfies the defining properties of a Brownian motion. These properties are:

1. **Initial Condition**: \( X_0 = 0 \).
2. **Independent Increments**: The increments \( X_t - X_s \) are independent for any \( s < t \).
3. **Stationary Increments**: The increments \( X_t - X_s \) have a distribution that depends only on \( t - s \).
4. **Continuity in Probability**: For any \( \epsilon > 0 \) and \( t_0 > 0 \), \( P(|X_t - X_{t_0}| > \epsilon) \to 0 \) as \( t \to t_0^+ \).

**1. Initial Condition:**

For \( t = 0 \):
\[
X_0 = 0 \cdot W_{1/0} = 0
\]
Hence, the initial condition holds.

**2. Independent Increments:**

Consider two time points \( s < t \). The increment:
\[
X_t - X_s = tW_{1/t} - sW_{1/s}
\]
Since \( W_t \) is a Brownian motion with independent increments, for any \( u \neq v \), \( W_u - W_v \) is independent of all increments before \( v \). 

To see the independence between \( X_t - X_s \) and \( X_u - X_v \) for disjoint intervals or non-overlapping time segments, consider that when \( s < t < u < v \):
\[
X_t - X_s = tW_{1/t} - sW_{1/s}
\]
and
\[
X_u - X_v = uW_{1/u} - vW_{1/v}
\]
The increments span different time intervals and are independent due to the properties of Brownian motion. Therefore, \( X_t - X_s \) is independent of all previous increments.

**3. Stationary Increments:**

For fixed interval length \( h = t - s > 0 \), we examine:
\[
X_{s+h} - X_s = (s + h)W_{1/(s + h)} - s W_{1/s}
\]
Simplify:
\[
= sW_{1/(s + h)} + h W_{1/(s + h)} - s W_{1/s}
\]
Factor out \( s \):
\[
= s (W_{1/(s + h)} - W_{1/s}) + h W_{1/(s + h)}
\]
The term \( W_{1/(s + h)} - W_{1/s} \) is independent of \( s \), and its variance scales with \( h \). However, when divided by the time interval \( h \), we have:
\[
\frac{X_{s+h} - X_s}{h} = \frac{s}{h} (W_{1/(s + h)} - W_{1/s}) + W_{1/(s + h)}
\]
The term involving \( s/h \) tends to zero as \( s \to 0 \), and the variance of this expression is proportional to \( h \). This shows that the distribution of increments depends only on \( h \), satisfying stationarity.

**4. Continuity in Probability:**

For any \( \epsilon > 0 \) and \( t_0 > 0 \), consider a sequence \( t_n \to t_0^+ \):
\[
P(|X_{t_n} - X_{t_0}| > \epsilon) = P\left(\left| (t_n + t_0)(W_{1/(t_n)} - W_{1/(t_0)}) \right| > \epsilon\right)
\]
Divide both sides by \( t_n + t_0 \):
\[
P\left(|W_{1/(t_n)} - W_{1/(t_0)}| > \frac{\epsilon}{t_n + t_0}\right)
\]
As \( t_n \to t_0^+ \), the time interval between \( 1/t_n \) and \( 1/t_0 \) becomes smaller, approaching zero. By the continuity of Brownian motion in probability:
\[
P(|W_{t} - W_{t_0}| > \epsilon') \to 0 \text{ as } t \to t_0^+
\]
for any \( t_0, \epsilon' > 0 \). Hence, \( X_t \) is continuous in probability.

**Conclusion:**

Since all the defining properties of a Brownian motion are satisfied by \( \{X_t = tW_{1/t},\ t > 0,\ X_0 = 0\} \), we conclude that this process is indeed a Brownian motion. Therefore, if \( W_t \) is a Brownian motion, then the process \( X_t \) defined as above is also a Brownian motion.

```lean4
theorem brownian_motion_iff (processes : 
    ℝ → ℝ → 𝓝 ↦ 𝔽) :
(∀ t ∈ ℝ^+ , processes t ∈ 𝔽 ∧ Gaussian measurable)
⇒ (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+ → (fun s ↦ processes s ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun s ↦ processes s ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun t ↦ processes t ∈ ℝ^+
          → (fun s ↦ processes s ∈ ℝ^+
          → BrownianMotion processes))) :=
    -- Assume the process satisfies all three conditions of a Brownian motion.
    filter_upwards fun s ↦ fun t ↦ Filter.has_nonempty_range (fun f ↦ (f 0 = 0 ∧ Gaussian measurable) f (by aesop [s, t]))
```
