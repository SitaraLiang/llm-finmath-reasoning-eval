Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process \( (X_t)_{t \geq 0} \) is a Brownian motion if and only if the following three conditions are satisfied: \( X_0 = 0 \), it is a centered Gaussian process, and the covariance between \( X_s \) and \( X_t \) is \( \min(s, t) \):

1. **Initial Condition**: The first condition \( X_0 = 0 \) is one of the defining properties of Brownian motion.

2. **Centered Gaussian Process**: A centered Gaussian process means every finite-dimensional distribution is a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector all zeros. This property is satisfied by Brownian motion because it is centered and each increment is normally distributed.

3. **Covariance Structure**: The covariance between \( X_s \) and \( X_t \) for any two times \( s \) and \( t \) is given by \( \min(s, t) \). This condition ensures that the increments are independent of their past values unless one time is less than or equal to the other. For example, if \( s < t \), then \( X_s \) and \( X_t - X_s \) are independent, making their covariance zero.

To prove the converse, we need to show that a process satisfying these conditions is indeed a Brownian motion:

1. **Initial Condition**: By definition, \( X_0 = 0 \).

2. **Centered Gaussian Process**: Since the process is centered and each increment is normally distributed, it is Gaussian.

3. **Covariance Structure**: The covariance structure matches that of Brownian motion, ensuring that all finite-dimensional distributions are jointly normal with mean zero and variance given by \( \min(s, t) \).

Thus, the conditions provided are equivalent to being a Brownian motion process.

\[
\boxed{X_0=0,\ (X_t)\text{ is a centered Gaussian process},\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)}
\]
