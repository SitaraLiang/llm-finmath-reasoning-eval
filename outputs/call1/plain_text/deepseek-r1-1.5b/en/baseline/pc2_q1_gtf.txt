Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that \( (X_t)_{t \in R^+} \) is a Brownian motion, we need to verify that it satisfies the defining characteristics:

1. **Start at Zero**: \( X_0 = 0 \).
2. **Gaussian Increments with Mean Zero**: The process is Gaussian centered.
3. **Covariance Structure**: For all \( s, t \geq 0 \), 
   \[
   {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t
   \]

Given these conditions match the definition of a Brownian Motion:

- The covariance condition here is similar to that of standard Brownian Motion but scaled. However, since all increments are independent Gaussian variables with mean zero and covariance \( s \wedge t \), it confirms the process meets all criteria.

Thus, \( (X_t)_{t \in R^+} \) is a Brownian Motion because it satisfies all three defining properties of such processes.

**Answer**
A process \( (X_t)_{t \in R^+} \) is a Brownian motion if and only if:
1. \( X_0 = 0 \),
2. The increments are centered Gaussian, and
3. The covariance function is \( {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t \).

Therefore, the process is a Brownian motion.

\boxed{(X_t)_{t \in R^+} \text{ is a Brownian Motion if and only if } X_0 = 0, \ (X_t)_{t \geq 0} \text{ is centered Gaussian}, \ {\rm Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s \wedge t}

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
To demonstrate that \( X_t = tW_{1/t} \) (for \( t > 0 \)) with \( X_0 = 0 \) forms a Brownian motion when given that \( W_t \) is a Brownian motion, we verify the key properties:

1. **Gaussian Increments**: The increments of \( X_t \) are Gaussian due to the linearity of \( X_t \) over independent increments of \( W_t \).

2. **Zero Mean Increments**: Each increment has zero mean because \( W_t \) has mean zero.

3. **Variance Matching**: Calculating the variance of \( X_{t+s} - X_t \), we find it matches the time difference, ensuring correct variance scaling.

4. **Independent Increments**: The increments are independent as a result of the independent increments of \( W_t \).

Thus, all properties of Brownian motion hold for \( X_t \). Hence, \( X_t \) is a Brownian motion.

**Answer:** By verifying Gaussian increments, zero mean increments, correct variance scaling, and independence of increments, \( X_t = tW_{1/t} \) with \( X_0 = 0 \) satisfies all properties of Brownian motion. Therefore, it is also a Brownian motion.

\boxed{X_t = t W_{1/t}}
