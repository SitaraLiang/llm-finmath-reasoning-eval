Question 1:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau)$.

Answer:
To compute \(\E(W_\tau)\) when \(\tau\) is a bounded stopping time, we can apply the **Optional Stopping Theorem (OST)**. 

Since \(W_t\) is a Brownian motion, it is a martingale with respect to its natural filtration. A bounded stopping time \(\tau\) ensures that \(W_\tau\) is integrable and well-defined. By the OST, for any bounded stopping time \(\tau\), we have:

\[
\E[W_\tau] = \E[W_0]
\]

Given that \(W_0 = 0\), it follows directly that:

\[
\E(W_\tau) = 0
\]

Thus, the expected value of \(W_\tau\) is zero.

\(\boxed{0}\)

Question 2:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau^2)$.

Answer:
To compute \( E(W_\tau^2) \) when \( \tau \) is a bounded stopping time, we can use the properties of Brownian motion and martingales.

1. **Martingale Property**: A Brownian motion \( W_t \) is a martingale with respect to its natural filtration. Since \( \tau \) is a bounded stopping time, the stopped process \( W_\tau \) is also a martingale.
   
2. **Optional Stopping Theorem (OST)**: Applying OST to the square process \( W_{\tau}^2 \), which is integrable and well-defined because \( \tau \) is bounded, we get:
   \[
   E[W_\tau^2] = E[\tau]
   \]

3. **Gaussian Distribution**: Since \( W_t \) is Gaussian at any stopping time \( \tau \), \( W_{\tau} \) is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance equal to the expectation of \( \tau \). Therefore, the expected value of its square is just the variance, which is \( E[\tau] \).

Thus, the expected value of \( W_\tau^2 \) is equal to the expectation of the stopping time \( \tau \).

\[
E[W_{\tau}^2] = \boxed{E[\tau]}
\]
