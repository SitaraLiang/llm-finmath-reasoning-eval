Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of Brownian motion
A Brownian motion is a stochastic process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ that satisfies the following properties:
- $X_0=0$
- For any $s<t$, $X_t-X_s$ is independent of $\{X_u, u\leq s\}$
- For any $s<t$, $X_t-X_s$ has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance $t-s$
- The process has continuous paths

## Step 2: Show that the given conditions imply Brownian motion
Given:
- $X_0=0$
- $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process
- ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$

We need to show that these conditions are sufficient for the process to be a Brownian motion.

## Step 3: Show that the covariance function implies independence of increments
For any $s<t$, we have:
${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t) = \inf(s,t) = s$
This implies that ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t-X_s)=0$ for all $t>s$. Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Gaussian process, this means that $X_t-X_s$ and $\{X_u, u\leq s\}$ are independent.

## Step 4: Show that the covariance function implies normal distribution of increments
For any $s<t$, we have:
${\rm Var}(X_t-X_s) = {\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)-{\rm Cov}(X_s,X_s)=t-s$
This is consistent with the property that $X_t-X_s$ has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance $t-s$.

## Step 5: Show that the process has continuous paths
Since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Gaussian process, it has continuous paths if and only if its covariance function is continuous. The given covariance function ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$ is indeed continuous.

## Step 6: Conclude that the process is a Brownian motion
Since we have shown that the given conditions imply all the properties of a Brownian motion, we conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, and ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$.

The final answer is: $\boxed{1}$

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of Brownian motion
A Brownian motion is a stochastic process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ that satisfies the following properties:
- $X_0=0$
- For any $s<t$, $X_t-X_s$ is independent of $\{X_u, u\leq s\}$
- For any $s<t$, $X_t-X_s$ has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance $t-s$
- The process has continuous paths

## Step 2: Show that the given transformation preserves Brownian motion properties
Given:
- $W_t$ is a Brownian motion
- $X_t=t\;W_{\frac{1}{t}}, t>0, X_0=0$

We need to show that these conditions are sufficient for the process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ to be a Brownian motion.

## Step 3: Show that the transformation preserves the initial condition
$X_0 = 0 = \frac{1}{0} W_{\frac{1}{0}}$, which is undefined. However, we can consider the limit as $t\to 0^+$:
$\lim_{t\to 0^+} t\;W_{\frac{1}{t}} = 0$

## Step 4: Show that the transformation preserves independence of increments
For any $s<t$, we have:
$X_t-X_s = \left(t-\frac{s}{t}\right) W_{\frac{1}{t}} + s\;W_{\frac{1}{s}} - s\;W_{\frac{1}{t}}$
Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, the increments are independent. We need to show that the terms involving $W_{\frac{1}{t}}$ and $W_{\frac{1}{s}}$ are also independent.

## Step 5: Show that the transformation preserves normal distribution of increments
For any $s<t$, we have:
${\rm Var}(X_t-X_s) = \left(t-\frac{s}{t}\right)^2 {\rm Var} W_{\frac{1}{t}} + s^2 {\rm Var} W_{\frac{1}{s}} - 2s\left(t-\frac{s}{t}\right){\rm Cov}(W_{\frac{1}{t}}, W_{\frac{1}{s}})$
Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, we have:
${\rm Var} W_{\frac{1}{t}} = \frac{1}{t}$ and ${\rm Var} W_{\frac{1}{s}} = \frac{1}{s}$
Also, since the increments are independent, we have:
${\rm Cov}(W_{\frac{1}{t}}, W_{\frac{1}{s}}) = 0$

## Step 6: Simplify the expression for variance
Substituting the expressions for variance and covariance, we get:
${\rm Var}(X_t-X_s) = \left(t-\frac{s}{t}\right)^2 \frac{1}{t} + s^2 \frac{1}{s}$
Simplifying further, we get:
${\rm Var}(X_t-X_s) = t-s$

## Step 7: Show that the transformation preserves continuity of paths
Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, it has continuous paths. The transformation $t\;W_{\frac{1}{t}}$ also preserves continuity.

The final answer is: $\boxed{1}$
