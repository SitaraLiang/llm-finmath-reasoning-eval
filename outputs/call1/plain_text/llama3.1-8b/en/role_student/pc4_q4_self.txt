Question 1:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau)$.

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of Brownian Motion
A Brownian motion $W_t$ has the property that for any $t \geq 0$, $\E[W_t] = 0$.

## Step 2: Apply the property to a bounded stopping time
Since $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, we can consider it as a random variable taking values in $[0,T]$ for some finite $T$. Then, by linearity of expectation and the fact that $\E[W_t] = 0$, we have:

$\E[W_\tau] = \E[\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau]]$

## Step 3: Use the law of iterated expectations
The inner expectation is taken with respect to the conditional distribution of $W_\tau$ given $\mathcal{F}_\tau$. Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, its increments are independent and normally distributed. Therefore:

$\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau] = W_{\tau^-}$

## Step 4: Apply the martingale property
Since $W_t$ is a martingale, we have $\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_{t<\tau}] = W_t$. Taking expectations and using the fact that $\tau$ is bounded:

$\E[\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau]] = \E[W_{\tau^-}]$

## Step 5: Use the martingale property again
Since $W_t$ is a martingale, we have $\E[W_{\tau^-}] = \E[W_0]$. But $\E[W_0] = W_0$, and since $W_0 = 0$ by definition of Brownian motion:

$\E[W_\tau] = 0$

The final answer is: $\boxed{0}$

Question 2:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau^2)$.

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of Brownian Motion
A Brownian motion $W_t$ has the property that for any $t \geq 0$, $\E[W_t] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(W_t) = t$.

## Step 2: Apply the property to a bounded stopping time
Since $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, we can consider it as a random variable taking values in $[0,T]$ for some finite $T$. Then, by linearity of expectation and the fact that $\E[W_t] = 0$, we have:

$\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) = \E[(W_\tau - \E[W_\tau])^2]$

## Step 3: Use the law of iterated expectations
The inner expectation is taken with respect to the conditional distribution of $W_\tau$ given $\mathcal{F}_\tau$. Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, its increments are independent and normally distributed. Therefore:

$\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) = \E[\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau)] + \mathrm{Var}(\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau])$

## Step 4: Apply the martingale property
Since $W_t$ is a martingale, we have $\E[W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_{t<\tau}] = W_t$. Taking expectations and using the fact that $\tau$ is bounded:

$\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) = \E[\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau | \mathcal{F}_\tau)]$

## Step 5: Use the property of Brownian motion
Since $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, its increments are independent and normally distributed. Therefore:

$\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) = \E[\tau]$

## Step 6: Compute $\E(W_\tau^2)$
Using the definition of variance and the fact that $\E[W_\tau] = 0$, we have:

$\E(W_\tau^2) = \mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) + (\E[W_\tau])^2$

## Step 7: Simplify the expression
Since $\E[W_\tau] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(W_\tau) = \E[\tau]$, we have:

$\E(W_\tau^2) = \E[\tau]$

The final answer is: $\boxed{\E[\tau]}$
