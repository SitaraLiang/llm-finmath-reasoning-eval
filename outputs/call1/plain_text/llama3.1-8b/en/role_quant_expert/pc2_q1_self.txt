Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of a Brownian motion
A Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a stochastic process that satisfies:
- $B_0 = 0$ almost surely,
- For all $s,t \geq 0$, $\mathbb{E}[B_s] = 0$ and $\text{Cov}(B_s,B_t) = \min(s,t)$,
- The increments are independent, i.e., for any $s < t < u$, $B_u - B_t$ is independent of $B_s - B_0$.

## Step 2: Show that if $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion, then it satisfies the given conditions
If $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion, then:
- $X_0 = B_0 = 0$ almost surely,
- Since $\mathbb{E}[B_s] = 0$, we have that $\mathbb{E}[X_s] = 0$ for all $s$. Hence, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process.
- By definition of Brownian motion, $\text{Cov}(B_s,B_t) = \min(s,t)$.

## Step 3: Show that if the given conditions are satisfied, then $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion
If $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is centered Gaussian, and $\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$, we need to show that the increments are independent.
Let $s < t < u$. Then:
$\mathbb{E}[X_u - X_t] = 0$ since $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is centered Gaussian,
$\text{Cov}(X_s,X_u - X_t) = \inf(s,u)-\inf(s,t)=0$ since $\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t)$.

## Step 4: Conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion
Since the increments of $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ are independent, centered Gaussian, and have covariance $\min(s,t)$, we conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.

The final answer is: $\boxed{1}$

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
## Step 1: Recall the definition of a Brownian motion
A Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a stochastic process that satisfies:
- $B_0 = 0$ almost surely,
- For all $s,t \geq 0$, $\mathbb{E}[B_s] = 0$ and $\text{Cov}(B_s,B_t) = \min(s,t)$,
- The increments are independent, i.e., for any $s < t < u$, $B_u - B_t$ is independent of $B_s - B_0$.

## Step 2: Show that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ satisfies the first condition
Since $X_0 = 0$, we have that $X_0=0$ almost surely.

## Step 3: Show that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is centered Gaussian
For any $s,t>0$, we have:
$\mathbb{E}[X_s] = sW_{\frac 1s}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac 1{\sqrt s} W_1\right]=0$ since $\mathbb{E}[W_1]=0$,
$\text{Var}(X_s) = \mathbb{E}\left[(sW_{\frac 1s})^2\right] - (\mathbb{E}[sW_{\frac 1s}])^2 = s^2\mathbb{E}\left[W_1^2\right]-0=s$ since $\text{Var}(W_1)=1$.

## Step 4: Show that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ satisfies the covariance condition
For any $s,t>0$, we have:
$\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t) = \mathbb{E}\left[(sW_{\frac 1s})(tW_{\frac 1t})\right] - \mathbb{E}[sW_{\frac 1s}]\mathbb{E}[tW_{\frac 1t}]$,
$\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t) = st\mathbb{E}\left[W_1^2\right]-0=st$ since $\text{Var}(W_1)=1$.

## Step 5: Show that the increments of $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ are independent
Let $s < t < u$. Then:
$\mathbb{E}[X_u - X_t] = (u-t)W_{\frac 1{ut}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac {u-t} {\sqrt{\frac 1{ut}}} W_1\right]=0$ since $\mathbb{E}[W_1]=0$,
$\text{Cov}(X_s,X_u - X_t) = \mathbb{E}\left[(sW_{\frac 1s})((u-t)W_{\frac 1{ut}})\right] - \mathbb{E}[sW_{\frac 1s}]\mathbb{E}[(u-t)W_{\frac 1{ut}}]$,
$\text{Cov}(X_s,X_u - X_t) = (su-t^2)\mathbb{E}\left[W_1^2\right]-0=0$ since $\text{Var}(W_1)=1$.

## Step 6: Conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion
Since the increments of $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ are independent, centered Gaussian, and have covariance $\min(s,t)$, we conclude that $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion.
