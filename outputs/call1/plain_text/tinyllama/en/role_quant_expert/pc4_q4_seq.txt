Question 1:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau)$.

Answer:
Let $W_t$ be a Brownian motion and assume that $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time defined as the last time that the process takes a step towards zero (i.e., an instantaneous stop-loss) before eventually settling at zero, where the initial position of the process is denoted by $w_0$.

From the definition of $\tau$, it follows that

\begin{align*}
\E[W_\tau] &= \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^{\tau}e^{-\int_0^s w_r^2dr}dr\right] \\
&= \int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \int_0^\infty e^{-y^2/2}dy \\
&= \int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx \cdot \frac{1}{2}e^{2\tau/\sqrt{2}} \\
&= \frac{\E[W_{\tau}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}dx,
\end{align*}
where we used the fundamental theorem of calculus and the fact that $e^{-\int_a^b f(z)dz}=1/2$ for all $f\in C^1([a,b])$ (i.e., a continuous function on $[a,b]$).

Hence,

\begin{align*}
\E[W_\tau] &= \frac{\E[W_{\tau}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}dx \\
&= \frac{\E[W_{\tau}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2} dx = \frac{\mathbb{E}[|W_\tau|^2]}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{2\tau/\sqrt{2}},
\end{align*}
where we used the fact that $e^{-\int_a^b f(z)dz}=1/2$ for all $f\in C^1([a,b])$ (i.e., a continuous function on $[a,b]$).

Thus,

\begin{align*}
\E(W_\tau) &= \frac{\mathbb{E}[|W_\tau|^2]}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{2\tau/\sqrt{2}} \\
&= \frac{\E[W^2_{\tau}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}\E[\tau]} = \frac{\E[W^2]}{\sqrt{4\pi^2}\E[\tau]}.
\end{align*}
Therefore,

\begin{align*}
\E(W_\tau) &= \E[|W_{\tau}|^2] \cdot \sqrt{4\pi^2}\,\E[\tau] \\
&= 2\E[|W_0|^2]\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx \cdot \sqrt{4\pi^2}\E[\tau] \\
& = 2\E[|W_0|^2] \cdot \frac{\sqrt{8\pi}\E[\tau]}{\E[|W_0|^2]} \\
& = 2\E[|W_0|^2]\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx,
\end{align*}
where we used the fact that the integral is taken over $[0,\infty)$ and the definition of $\tau$ in terms of $w_0$. This completes the proof.
