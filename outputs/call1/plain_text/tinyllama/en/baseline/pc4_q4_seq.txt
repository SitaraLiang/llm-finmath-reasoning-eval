Question 1:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau)$.

Answer:
Let $T_n = \inf\{t>0\mid W_t\in B_n\}$, where $B_n$ denotes the $n$-th Boltzmann's particle, be a Brownian motion with the initial condition $B_0 = 0$. We have

$$W_\tau^2 = E[W_\tau|\mathcal{F}_\tau] + \int_{\tau}^T W_t\, dW_t$$

where $\mathcal{F}_\tau$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $W_1,\ldots,W_{n-1},W_\tau,W_n,W_{n+1},\ldots,W_T$. By applying the Ito formula to the last term in the formula above, we have

$$\E(W_\tau^2) = E[W_\tau^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau] + \int_{\tau}^T \left(V(\tau,X_t|W_t)\right)^2\, dt$$

where $V$ is the drift of $W$, and $X_t$ is a Brownian motion with $X_0 = 0$. We can use the Fokker-Planck equation to derive $E[W_\tau^2| \mathcal{F}_\tau]$:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \dot{\xi} + \sigma\left(\dot{\xi}-\xi-\dot{X}_t+Z_t\right) = 0$$

where $v(t,\xi)$, $z(t,\xi)$, and $W(t,x)$ are the mean-field potential, drift, and Brownian noise fields respectively. We can then apply the chain rule to $V$ and obtain:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \dot{\xi} + \sigma\left(\dot{\xi}-\dot{X}_t+Z_t\right) = -E[W(T,X_0)]^2$$

Therefore,

$$\E[V(W_\tau)^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau] = E[(W_\tau)^2 | \mathcal{F}_{\tau}] + E[\sigma^2(W_\tau)|\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]^2 + \int_{\tau}^T \left(V(\tau,X_t|W_t)\right)^2\, dt$$

Integrating both sides of the equation above with respect to $dt$ and using $\E[Z_t] = 0$, we get

$$\E[V(W_\tau)^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau] = E[(W_\tau)^2 | \mathcal{F}_{\tau}]^2 + \int_{\tau}^T \left(v(t,\xi)\right)^2\, dt$$

where $v(t,\xi)$ is the mean-field potential. Now we can write:

$$\E[W_\tau^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau] = E[\sum_{n=1}^\infty (T_n - \tau)^2 W_n^2| \mathcal{F}_\tau]$$

since $W_{\tau+1} = W_\tau + \int_{\tau}^T W_t\, dW_t$. Substituting this result into the equation above, we have:

$$\E(W_\tau^2) = E[W_\tau^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau] + E[\sum_{n=1}^\infty (T_n - \tau)^2 W_n^2| \mathcal{F}_\tau]$$

which is the quadratic variation of $W$. Finally, we get:

$$\E(W_\tau^2) = \frac{\tau^2}{4}\E[\sum_{n=1}^\infty (T_n - \tau)^4] + E[W_\tau^2 | \mathcal{F}_\tau]$$

which is the quadratic variation of $W$ under the assumption that $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time.

Question 2:
When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, compute $\E(W_\tau^2)$.

Answer:
In the context of a financial mathematics exercise on the problem of evaluating the expectation of a square-integrable random variable with a continuous (and increasing) probability distribution over a finite interval, we have:

Global assumptions:
1. $W_t$ is a Brownian Motion.

Local assumptions:
1. $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time (i.e., $\sigma$-algebra of sets).

Question 2: When $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, the expectation of $W_\tau^2$ can be computed as follows:

$$\E(W_\tau^2) = \int_0^\infty x^2 P(W \leq x) dx = \frac{1}{4}\sigma^2(\tau)$$

Here, we used the fact that $P(W \leq x)$ is a probability density function (PDF), which satisfies $\int_0^\infty x P(W \leq x)dx=1$, and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^2 P(W \leq x) dx=4$.

Furthermore, the integral in question can be evaluated by using the fundamental theorem of calculus to take the limit as $x\rightarrow 0^+$:
$$\lim_{\abs{x} \to 0^+}\frac{1}{x^2}\int_0^\infty x P(W \leq x)dx = \lim_{\abs{x} \to 0^+}\frac{\sigma^2(\tau)}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=\sqrt{\tau}} \frac{P(W\leq z)}{z^2} dz = 4\int_0^\infty P(W\leq x)dx$$

Substituting into the previous integral, we have:
$$\E(W_\tau^2)=\frac{1}{4}\sigma^2(\tau)$$

Therefore, the expected square of a square-integrable random variable with a continuous probability distribution over a finite interval can be expressed as the expectation of its squared value.
