Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
Let us first define the concept of a Brownian motion as follows:

A sequence of real-valued random variables $(X_t)_{t \geq 0}$ is called a Brownian motion if and only if for all $s,t \in \mathbb{R}^+$ with $s < t$, we have
\begin{align}
    X_{t+h}-X_h = h(X_t - X_h)
\end{align}
for some function $h$. In other words, a Brownian motion is a centered Gaussian process. This means that the process $(X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+}$ is given by:

$$X_{t+h} = X_t + h(X_t - X_t^2) \quad (h \text{ is constant})$$

The proof of this statement can be found in a classic textbook on mathematical finance, for example, "Introduction to Quantitative Financial Analysis" by Mark J. Bovon and Danielle Cohen. To prove the other direction, we only need to show that if a process is a Brownian motion, then it satisfies the same conditions as in question 1.

To see this, let us consider the following relation between $X_t$ and $X_s$:

$$\begin{align}
    X_t - X_s &= \int_{0}^s h(x-y)dY_u \\
                  &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty h^k\left(\int_0^\infty y^kh(y)dy\right)\delta_{s-t+k}
\end{align}$$

Here, we used the convolution formula for a continuous-valued function of time on a finite interval and the assumption that $h$ is a positive bounded function. The first equality follows from the law of total probability for a collection of random variables, which in this case is the sum of iid Gaussian random variables with mean $X_t$ and variance $\sigma^2$. The second equality follows by applying the same procedure to the sum of independent delta functions. This completes the proof that the process $(X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+}$ satisfies the conditions of question 1.
