Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process \( (X_t)_{t \geq 0} \) is a Brownian motion if and only if it satisfies the following conditions: 

1. \( X_0 = 0 \),
2. \( (X_t) \) is a centered Gaussian process,
3. The covariance function is \( \text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \min(s, t) \),

we need to verify both directions.

**Forward Direction:**

Assuming \( (X_t) \) is a Brownian motion:
1. By definition, \( X_0 = 0 \).
2. Brownian motion has independent and identically distributed increments that are Gaussian, making the entire process a centered Gaussian process.
3. The covariance between \( X_s \) and \( X_t \) for Brownian motion is \( \min(s, t) \).

**Converse Direction:**

Assuming conditions 1, 2, and 3 hold:
1. \( X_0 = 0 \).
2. The process is a centered Gaussian process with all finite-dimensional distributions being multivariate normal.
3. The covariance structure \( \text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \min(s, t) \) implies that the increments are uncorrelated and hence independent (since jointly normal variables with zero covariance are independent).
4. Each increment \( X_t - X_s \) has variance \( t - s \), making it a Gaussian process with stationary and independent increments.

Thus, all conditions for a Brownian motion are satisfied: starting at zero, having independent increments, and each increment being Gaussian with variance equal to the time difference. Therefore, the process is a Brownian motion.

\[
\boxed{X_t \text{ is a Brownian motion}}
\]

Question 2:
Show that if $W_t$ is a Brownian motion, $ \{X_t=t\;W_{\frac 1t}, t>0, X_0=0 \}$ is a Brownian motion too.

Answer:
To show that \( X_t = t W_{1/t} \) for \( t > 0 \) with \( X_0 = 0 \) is a Brownian motion when \( W_t \) is a Brownian motion, we verify the following properties:

1. **Starts at zero**: \( X_0 = 0 \cdot W_{\infty} = 0 \).

2. **Gaussian process with mean zero**: Since \( W_{1/t} \) is Gaussian for each fixed \( t > 0 \), scaling by \( t \) preserves normality, so \( X_t \) is Gaussian with mean 0.

3. **Covariance structure**: For any \( s, t > 0 \),
   \[
   \text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \text{Cov}(s W_{1/s}, t W_{1/t}) = st \cdot \min\left(\frac{1}{s}, \frac{1}{t}\right) = \min(s, t).
   \]

4. **Variance**: For each \( t > 0 \),
   \[
   \text{Var}(X_t) = \text{Var}(t W_{1/t}) = t^2 \cdot \frac{1}{t} = t.
   \]

5. **Independent increments**: The finite-dimensional distributions of \( X_t \) are multivariate normal with covariance matrix corresponding to the min function, ensuring independence of increments.

6. **Continuity**: Since \( W_{1/t} \) has continuous paths and scaling by \( t \) preserves continuity, \( X_t \) also has continuous paths.

Thus, all conditions for a Brownian motion are satisfied.

\boxed{X_t \text{ is a Brownian motion}}
