Question 1:
Show that a process $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a Brownian motion if and only if: $X_0=0$, $(X_t)_{t\in \R^+}$ is a centered Gaussian process, ${\rm Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\inf(s,t).$

Answer:
To show that a process $(X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}^+}$ is a **Brownian motion** if and only if the following conditions hold:

- $X_0 = 0$,
- $(X_t)$ is a **centered Gaussian process**,
- $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \inf(s, t)$,

we proceed as follows:

---

### **Step 1: Understanding the Conditions**

- **Condition 1**: $X_0 = 0$  
  This is a fundamental property of Brownian motion. In standard Brownian motion, the process starts at 0, so this condition is satisfied.

- **Condition 2**: $(X_t)$ is a **centered Gaussian process**  
  This means that the process $(X_t)$ is a Gaussian process with mean 0 and zero covariance between any two points $s$ and $t$ (i.e., $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = 0$ for $s \neq t$).

- **Condition 3**: $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \inf(s, t)$  
  This condition defines the covariance function of the process. For $s \geq t$, $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = s$, and for $t \geq s$, $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = t$. This matches the standard definition of a Brownian motion.

---

### **Step 2: Verifying the Process is a Brownian Motion**

We now show that a process satisfying the above conditions is a Brownian motion.

#### **1. Independent Increments**

- For $s > t$, $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = t$, which implies that the process has independent increments. This is a key property of Brownian motion.

#### **2. Gaussian Process**

- The process $(X_t)$ being a centered Gaussian process implies that it is a Gaussian process with mean 0 and zero covariance between any two points $s$ and $t$ (i.e., $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = 0$).

#### **3. Covariance Function**

- The condition $\text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \inf(s, t)$ ensures that the process has a covariance function that is consistent with the standard Brownian motion properties.

---

### **Conclusion**

Since the process satisfies all three conditions, it follows that:

$$
\boxed{X_0 = 0 \text{ and } \text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \inf(s, t)}
$$

This process is a **Brownian motion**.

To show that the process $ X_t = t \cdot W_{\frac{1}{t}} $ is a Brownian motion, we proceed as follows:

1. **Assumptions**:

2. **Increment Analysis**:

   Therefore, $ Y_t - Y_{t-1} $ is a process with independent and identically distributed increments, which implies that $ Y_t $ is a Brownian motion.

3. **Conclusion**:  
   The process $ X_t = t \cdot W_{\frac{1}{t}} $ satisfies the properties of a Brownian motion: it is centered, has independent and identically distributed increments, and is a continuous process. Thus, $ X_t $ is a Brownian motion.
